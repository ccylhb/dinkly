You do not get to choose how long your agent takes. You do get to choose what happens in the gap.

The problem with conventional break advice is scale. "Go for a walk" needs thirty minutes you do not have. "Take a proper lunch" is a different day. What you actually have is a ragged, unpredictable series of ninety-second-to-three-minute holes, and most advice simply does not fit through them.

So here is a menu built for the hole, not the day. Every item is under three minutes, needs no equipment you do not already own, and has a definite end.

## Body (pick one)

**1. The far look.** Twenty seconds staring at something more than six metres away. Your focusing muscles spend the whole day clenched at monitor distance; this is the release.

**2. The long exhale.** In for four, out for eight, three times. The only physiological trick on this list with a reliable effect, and it works in under thirty seconds.

**3. Both feet down.** Sit back properly. Feet flat, spine supported, shoulders unhooked from your ears. Hold it for a minute rather than "fixing your posture" forever.

**4. The cold wrists.** Thirty seconds of cold water on your wrists and the back of your neck. Absurdly effective when you are foggy, and takes less time than making tea.

**5. The corridor round-trip.** Walk to the furthest wall you can reach in thirty seconds, touch it, come back. No phone. The touch is the point — it turns a shuffle into a journey.

## Head (pick one)

**6. The unread tab close.** Close three tabs you were never going to read. This is not tidying; it is removing three small unpaid debts from your attention.

**7. One honest sentence.** Write one sentence in a place nobody will read. Do not edit it. Close it. This is a drain valve, not a journaling practice.

**8. The two-minute task.** There is one thing you have been walking past for a week. Do that specific thing and stop. Momentum is built out of finished scraps.

**9. Name the thing you are carrying.** A grudge, a draft, a worry about Thursday. Say it out loud, then put it down. You can pick it up later; the card only says you may also not.

## Play (pick one)

**10. Pull one card.** A [daily draw](/daily-draw/) with a clear instruction and a DO/DON'T list. Ninety seconds, zero decisions.

**11. Read one joke.** Setups and punchlines, one click apart. Ten seconds each, and genuinely funny ones exist. See the [Joke Jar](/jokes/).

**12. Clear four pairs.** Not the whole memory board — just four pairs. A finished sub-task beats an unfinished main task for mood, and mood is the actual currency here.

## What to avoid

There is a short list of things that look like breaks and are not:

- **The feed.** Infinite, engineered to not end, and it will eat three minutes and hand back thirty in change.
- **Email.** This is a change of workload. It is not a rest, and it generates new open loops.
- **"Quickly checking" anything.** The word "quickly" in a break description is a warning label.
- **Doing a second task.** You already have a task. Starting a second one during the wait is how you end up with five.

The common thread: breaks should **end**. Anything that cannot end is not a break.

## Building the habit

Do not try to do all twelve. Pick two — one for the body, one for the head — and use them consistently for a week. Consistency beats variety here, because the point is to make the *pause* automatic rather than to optimise the pause itself.

And set a real rule: **when the task finishes, you stop the break, not the other way around.** The break serves the work. That relationship only stays healthy if it is explicit.

The whole system costs you three minutes. It buys back an afternoon.
