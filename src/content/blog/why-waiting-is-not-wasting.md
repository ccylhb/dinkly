There is a specific kind of guilt that comes with watching a progress bar. You are not working. You are not resting. You are *supervising*, which looks like work from the outside and feels like nothing from the inside.

So you do what everyone does: you open a second tab, start a small task, and now you have five half-finished things instead of one that is nearly done.

This essay is an argument for the opposite. Not "be more productive with your waiting" — that is the same trap in a nicer outfit. The argument is simpler: **a wait you cannot shorten should be spent on something you can actually finish.**

## The stop-start problem

Human attention has two settings, and they are expensive to switch between. When you interrupt focused work with a partially-related task, you pay what researchers call a *switch cost* — a measurable penalty that shows up as slower performance and more errors on both sides of the interruption.

The critical detail: a wait is a **forced** interruption. You did not choose it. Which means you were going to pay the switching cost either way. The only question is what you buy with it.

There are three options on the table:

1. **Watch the progress indicator.** Costs attention, returns nothing. The wait ends when it ends.
2. **Start something new.** Costs attention, returns a sixth unfinished project.
3. **Do something small and complete.** Costs the same interruption you were already paying, returns a finished thing and a slightly different mood.

Option 3 is the only one where the ledger balances.

## What the break itself should look like

Short breaks work, but not in the ways people assume. The consistent finding across the literature is that *micro-breaks* — under about five minutes — are most effective when they do one of three things:

- **Reduce visual load.** Your eyes have been locked to a fixed distance for a while. Looking at something far away for twenty seconds is not a myth; it is the cheapest intervention available to you.
- **Change physical posture.** Almost any movement beats no movement, and the chair you are sitting in makes certain postures inevitable.
- **Give a clean, small win.** A joke read. A card drawn. A game finished. A one-sentence note written.

Notice what is *not* on that list: checking email, opening a feed, or anything with an infinite scroll. Those are not breaks. They are a change of workload, and they leave you exactly as tired as you started.

## The three-minute rule of thumb

A practical heuristic: if the thing cannot be finished in about three minutes, it is not a break. It is a new task wearing a break's clothing.

Three minutes is roughly the length of a short model response. It is long enough to do something real — read a joke, pull a card, match eight pairs of emoji, breathe deliberately for ten cycles — and short enough that coming back does not require a running start.

The other useful rule: **breaks should have an end.** An unbounded break is indistinguishable from procrastination, and it will make you feel worse than not having taken one at all. A finish line is the whole difference.

## Permission, not optimisation

Here is the part that took me the longest to accept: the value of a micro-break is not that it increases your output later. Sometimes it does. But framing the break as an investment is a way of keeping the productivity ledger open, and that ledger is the thing making you tired.

The better framing is that you are a person sitting in a chair, and the machine is going to take ninety seconds no matter what you do. You may spend those ninety seconds being a person.

That is not optimisation. It is closer to maintenance, and it is entirely legitimate.

## What to do right now

If your agent is currently thinking:

1. Look at something more than six metres away for twenty seconds.
2. Stand up. Even briefly. Even reluctantly.
3. Do one small thing with a clear end. [Pull today's card](/daily-draw/), [read a joke](/jokes/), [clear four pairs](/games/dink-match/).
4. Come back. Your work is exactly where you left it.

The spinner spins. You, my friend, rest.
